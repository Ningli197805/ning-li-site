NOV 2026 · TEN YEARS OF RUNNING

I have never seen Shenzhen at night, yet I have run all eight World Marathon Majors

— From 200 jin to eight stars: my ten years of running

During all the years I have lived in Shenzhen, I have rarely taken time to appreciate the city at night.

It sounds a little hard to believe.

Shenzhen is a vibrant city, but my life is simple: early to bed, early to rise, running and work. I do not socialise much either. Apart from family and colleagues, the people I spend most time with are a group of friends who love running.

Because I need to run early the next morning, I usually go to bed very early.

Some people may wonder whether life like this is a little monotonous.

For me, however, this is exactly what ten years of running has brought: a more regular life, greater inner calm, and better physical and mental health.

If I rewind to 2016, I find it hard to imagine that I would one day live like this.

Even less could I have imagined that someone who once weighed 200 jin (about 100 kilograms), sat in an office all day and hardly exercised would one day run in all eight cities of the World Marathon Majors.

In May 2026, I completed my eighth World Marathon Major star in Cape Town, South Africa.

According to the data I saw at the time on the official results platform, there were 117 Chinese runners in the first group to complete all eight stars. I was 38th among Chinese men and 44th overall.

I felt very honoured to be part of that group.

But when I look back on ten years of running, I realise that what is more precious than the eight stars is the change that took place, little by little, over those ten years.

The first 50 numbers

In 2016, I began to run seriously.

By then I had been working for many years. Like many people with desk jobs, a busy schedule, irregular routines and a lack of exercise had once pushed my weight to 200 jin.

My initial reason for running was simple: I wanted to lose some weight and feel healthier.

I knew very well that I was not naturally a sporty person.

To avoid losing interest after a few days, I set myself a small goal:

Complete 50 runs in 20 weeks.

After every run, I checked in on WeChat Moments and gave the run a number.

No.1, No.2, No.3...

With those numbers, I completed my first 50 runs.

Looking back, the method was very simple, but it genuinely helped me build an exercise habit.

Later, I began gradually increasing the distance.

In 2018, I ran my first half marathon.

By 2019, I had completed 12 half marathons in total.

At the time, completing 21.0975 kilometres already felt like a great achievement. I did not yet dare to attempt a full marathon of 42.195 kilometres.

Let alone take part in the World Marathon Majors.

I could never have imagined that ten years later I would still be numbering my marathons.

It is just that the story behind every number is now completely different.

One person's start, a community's companionship

Around 2018, I joined the Yuandadu Champions Club.

At that time, Yuandadu mainly held in-person training in Beijing. I was in Shenzhen, so I could only join the virtual running group and follow the club's training schedule.

Later, I made two special trips to Beijing to attend the club's in-person courses.

Those two experiences were the first time I deeply appreciated the importance of scientific training.

In the past, I thought running was simple: put on your running shoes and go out. If you worked hard enough and ran enough, your results would naturally improve.

Only after I began systematic training did I gradually understand that running is far more than that.

Training at different intensities, long runs, speed sessions, recovery and the arrangement of training cycles all serve a purpose.

During that period of growth, there is one person I particularly want to thank.

Her name is Liu Ying.

We have been close friends for 20 years, and we used to be colleagues.

If running was a choice I made for myself, Liu Ying was the person who kept reminding me, encouraging me and guiding me step by step towards marathon running.

From watching me start running, to completing half marathons, to taking on full marathons, she witnessed my growth all the way and kept giving me advice.

I used to love taking photos while running.

Whenever I saw a beautiful view, I wanted to stop and take a few pictures.

Liu Ying often reminded me:

"Serious runners do not stop to take photos."

Now it seems quite amusing.

Of course, that did not mean runners cannot take photos. She wanted me to treat training more seriously and avoid disrupting the rhythm of a session by stopping whenever I felt like it.

Later, my marathon time stayed around 3:30 for almost two years.

No matter how hard I tried, it seemed difficult to break through.

Only then did I truly realise that running a lot does not necessarily mean running well. Without scientific, systematic training, hard work alone may leave you at the same level for a long time.

Liu Ying gave me a great deal of advice, and we also raced and trained at altitude together.

She was not only my running friend, but also a very important guide in my running life.

In 2020, the Yuandadu Shenzhen Club was officially established.

I became its first member.

From then on, people affectionately called me "First Brother."

The nickname has carried on to this day and has become the club's established way of addressing me.

To be honest, I am not the fastest runner in the club.

But I have always treasured the name, because it represents not only the order in which I joined the club, but also my deep bond with this community.

Over the years, my marathon time improved from 4:06 to under four hours, then 3:30 and 3:20, and eventually to 3:05:30.

Every stage of progress depended on the professional guidance of the club's coaches and on teammates who trained and encouraged one another.

One person can start running through determination, but to keep running takes more than persistence: it takes method and a group of like-minded people.

The final five kilometres in Wuxi: keep moving even when the finish is out of sight

In 2019, after completing 12 half marathons, I began seriously considering a full marathon.

Again it was Liu Ying who encouraged me to aim further.

If you are already preparing for a full marathon, why not try the World Marathon Majors?

So I entered the 2020 London Marathon.

According to the original plan, London was supposed to be my first full marathon.

Looking back now, it was quite a bold decision.

Someone who had never run a full marathon was already thinking about running London.

But that is sometimes how a dream begins.

Then the pandemic disrupted the plan.

The London Marathon was postponed, and I kept my place. My marathon debut therefore became the Wuxi Marathon in November 2020.

That year I was 42.

Challenging 42.195 kilometres for the first time, I was unfamiliar with marathon racing and had never truly experienced how hard the second half could be.

After completing the earlier part of the course, I began to feel clearly exhausted at around kilometre 37.

There were still more than five kilometres to the finish.

To me now, five kilometres is just an ordinary training distance.

But to the version of me running his first marathon, those final kilometres felt extraordinarily long.

Because I did not know the course well, I had no idea how many bends lay ahead or where the finish was.

Every kilometre felt like a struggle.

My body was exhausted, but the race was not over.

That feeling suddenly made me think: is life not sometimes like this too?

Often we work towards a goal without knowing how much farther we have to go, or when we will truly arrive.

Sometimes we cannot even see where hope lies ahead.

Even so, we still have to keep moving forward.

Step by step. Hold on.

At last, I crossed the finish line.

4:06.

This was the result of my first full marathon.

After crossing the line, I cried.

The tears were not only a release after physical exhaustion, but also because I had finally completed something I once believed impossible.

From the struggle that began at kilometre 37 to finally crossing the finish, I felt more deeply than ever that marathon running and life have a great deal in common.

Sometimes, even when you cannot see the finish, you must believe that you are getting closer, one step at a time.

That Wuxi Marathon made me truly fall in love with the full marathon, and made me begin to believe that there might be more possibilities ahead.

And the London Marathon place I had originally planned to use in 2020 remained in reserve.

At the time, I did not know that four years later it would become the final stop in my first round of the six World Marathon Majors.

210 days, six cities, six stars

On 24 September 2023, I stood on the start line of the Berlin Marathon.

It was my first World Marathon Major.

Before that, I had always seen the World Marathon Majors as a distant dream. Berlin was where that dream began turning into real races, one after another.

After that, I took part in:

  1. September 2023, Berlin Marathon
  2. October 2023, Chicago Marathon
  3. November 2023, New York City Marathon
  4. March 2024, Tokyo Marathon
  5. April 2024, Boston Marathon
  6. April 2024, London Marathon

From Berlin, the first stop, to London, the last, only 210 days passed.

In less than seven months, I completed all six races then in the World Marathon Majors.

It was an extremely intense journey.

Different countries, different cities, different courses, long-haul flights, jet lag, and the physical toll of racing back-to-back.

In particular, in April 2024, Boston and London were only six days apart.

For a marathon runner, recovery after one race normally takes time, let alone while travelling between countries.

But I enjoyed every part of the journey.

Long flights and jet lag often prevented me from producing my best times, but every time I stood on a course I had previously seen only in photographs and videos, I still felt excited.

I could finally experience those cities as a runner.

And the 2024 London Marathon carried another meaning for me.

It was the very race I had entered in 2019 and originally planned to run in 2020.

The place I had waited four years to use was finally redeemed there.

When I finished London, I completed my first round of the six World Marathon Majors.

From first imagining a World Marathon Major to actually completing the goal took five years.

Some dreams need a little luck.

But more often they need time, preparation, and the refusal to give up throughout a long wait.

The Chinese flag one kilometre before the Berlin finish

One image from my first round of the six majors remains vivid.

It was the 2023 Berlin Marathon.

With about one kilometre to the finish, my wife Bingbing stood beside the course and handed me a Chinese flag.

The moment I took the flag, I felt overwhelmed.

In that instant, I suddenly felt that I was no longer fighting alone.

In the past, I focused more on my own result, pace and whether I could finish.

But when I received a Chinese flag on an overseas marathon course, I felt something special.

I was not only running for my own dream. I also felt a sense of belonging to my country, carrying the support of my family, friends and the Yuandadu running group as I gave my best on an overseas course.

That flag gave the race a different meaning in my mind.

It reminded me that although a marathon ultimately depends on your own feet, there are many people supporting you behind you.

From that moment on, I felt that my running was no longer just my own business.

And Bingbing was the person who placed that strength in my hands before the finish.

For the 2024 London Marathon, she flew to Britain especially to cheer for me.

By 2026, when I ran Boston for the second time, she even checked the course a day early to find the best place to cheer.

These are small things.

There was no dramatic plot and no grand speech.

But precisely these little details made me feel especially warm.

Anyone who runs marathons knows that a race is 42.195 kilometres, but preparing for those 42.195 kilometres often takes months.

Training again and again, waking early again and again, long runs at weekends, and all kinds of arrangements needed for a race.

These things not only take my own time, but also require understanding and support from my family.

So although finishing a marathon ultimately depends on my own feet, I have never felt that the results belong to me alone.

My parents, family, friends, and the coaches and teammates of Yuandadu all gave me a great deal of support along the way.

Bingbing in particular.

Often she does not need to say anything. Simply knowing that she is there makes me feel grounded.

A marathon is an individual 42.195 kilometres, but I have never run all the way to today alone.

Going higher in search of a breakthrough

After completing the six World Marathon Majors, I did not feel that my running goals were complete.

Instead, I began thinking more carefully about a question:

After so many years of running, and as I grow older, do I still have a chance to keep improving?

I was unwilling to remain at my old personal best.

Especially after a long period without a breakthrough, I increasingly understood that progress requires continual learning and continual adjustment of training methods.

From 2025 to 2026, while preparing for the Barossa, Shanghai and Seoul marathons, I made three trips to train at altitude in Yunnan.

The first was to Qujing.

At around 1,800 metres, I stayed for 35 days.

The second was Chuxiong, at 1,800 to 1,900 metres, where I trained for more than 30 days.

The third was Lijiang, at about 2,400 metres, where I stayed for nearly four weeks.

Altitude training feels different from running on the plains.

The partial pressure of oxygen in the air is lower, and the body responds differently to training stress. This required me to plan intensity more carefully, monitor recovery, and not simply chase distance and speed.

At the same time, I managed diet and weight more strictly, trying to make each preparation block more scientific.

During that period, my understanding of marathon training deepened again.

Running was no longer just about going out to cover a certain number of kilometres. It became a balance among training, nutrition, recovery, weight management and everyday habits.

Building on long-term systematic training, I combined altitude work with race preparation and set new personal bests at Barossa in 2025 and Seoul in 2026.

I could clearly feel the progress produced by that stage of training.

Of course, improvement is difficult to attribute entirely to any one training method. Long-term accumulation, a sensible training cycle, physical condition and race-day execution all matter equally.

But the altitude-training experiences made me more convinced of one thing:

Getting older does not mean we must accept decline. As long as we are willing to learn and train seriously, there is still a chance to keep improving.

The final two kilometres in Barossa: one second faster is still faster

On 24 August 2025, I ran the Barossa Marathon in Australia.

It was a very important race in my running life.

Because I had a goal I had kept in mind: to earn a Boston Marathon qualifying time, or BQ, through my own performance.

Boston has always held a special attraction for me.

Although I had run Boston through a charity place in 2024, I still hoped that one day I could return to that course on the strength of a time I had run myself.

The BQ standard for my age group was 3:15.

Because competition for places is fierce, actually earning entry often requires running faster than the standard.

My personal best at the time was around 3:14.

I knew I needed another breakthrough.

For that race I prepared thoroughly and specifically arranged altitude training and diet management.

On race day I worked hard to hold my own rhythm.

With two kilometres to go, I judged that there was a good chance I would earn Boston qualification.

But I did not relax.

At that moment, I had only one thought:

One second faster is one second faster; a minute faster is even better.

So even after more than 40 kilometres, I wanted to use every last bit of energy.

In the end, I crossed the finish in 3:06:50.

I had taken more than seven minutes off my personal best.

The breakthrough meant a great deal to me.

Because it not only set a new personal best, but finally gave me the chance to return to Boston on my own merit.

Just seven days later, on 31 August 2025, I ran the Sydney Marathon.

That was the year Sydney officially became the seventh World Marathon Major.

So one week after setting a personal best, I completed my seventh star.

Those two races became two of my most memorable experiences of 2025.

But what I remember most is the thought I had in the final two kilometres in Barossa.

When I knew I would most likely achieve my goal, I could easily have eased off.

Yet I still wanted to go faster.

Because I increasingly feel that the greatest pleasure in running is not simply completing a set goal.

It is still being willing to seek a little more progress when the goal is already within reach.

Two Bostons, two ways of arriving

If I had to choose the story that best represents my personal growth from all these years of marathoning, Boston would certainly be part of it.

I have run the Boston Marathon twice.

The first was in April 2024.

At that time I gained entry as a charity runner and donated USD 8,500.

I already felt very fortunate simply to stand on the Boston course.

Boston has a long history and is known for its strict qualifying standards. For many amateur runners, earning a BQ is a proud achievement.

After my first Boston, I set myself a new goal:

Next time, I want to come back on my own time.

That goal was not as easy as I imagined.

From 3:30 to 3:20 to 3:14, every improvement was backed by a long process of training.

I experienced plateaus and kept adjusting my training.

Then, in August 2025, I ran 3:06:50 in Barossa and finally earned my place for the 2026 Boston Marathon on time.

On 15 March 2026, I improved my personal best again at the Seoul Marathon, running 3:05:30.

A month later, on 20 April 2026, I returned to the Boston start line.

This time I was racing as a BQ runner.

The race had another special meaning.

It was the final race in my second round of the six World Marathon Majors.

My first round took less than seven months.

This time, returning to Boston was no longer just about collecting the six stars. I came back with the growth from several years of training to experience the course again.

The atmosphere at Boston was extraordinary.

The cheers and shouts from spectators along the course made the city's love of the marathon tangible.

Especially as I headed for the finish, the roar of support around me made me feel completely part of the occasion.

In that moment, the fatigue, tension and excitement of the race seemed to merge.

I no longer thought about my time or how many kilometres I had run.

I simply wanted to enjoy an unforgettable moment.

When I crossed the Boston finish line again, I was deeply moved.

Because I knew it was more than completing Boston for a second time.

It was also the perfect conclusion to my second round of the six majors.

The Boston course had not changed, but I was no longer the same person who first came here.

The first time, I fulfilled my Boston dream through a charity place.

The second time, I returned on a BQ earned through years of training.

I do not see one way as superior to the other.

The first gave me the chance to experience Boston; the second showed me the progress created by years of effort.

Both are precious experiences to me.

But crossing the line the second time carried extraordinary meaning.

It was both the fulfilment of a dream again and an answer sheet for years of hard work.

My first Boston showed me what a dream looked like; my second Boston showed me the power of growth.

Beyond eight stars, life is still on the road

In May 2026, I completed my eighth World Marathon Major star in Cape Town, South Africa.

From Berlin in September 2023 to Cape Town in May 2026, I completed races in all eight cities of the World Marathon Majors.

If finishing my first round of the six majors in 2024 fulfilled a dream I had held for years,

and crossing the Boston finish for the second time in 2026 affirmed years of training,

then the eighth star in Cape Town made me feel even more vividly that ten years of persistence had truly changed my life.

Ten years ago, I started running in order to lose weight.

At the time, I needed social-media check-ins to keep myself going.

Ten years later, running is no longer a task I have to force myself to complete. It is a natural part of life.

In 2026, I consistently maintain a monthly volume of around 350 to 450 kilometres.

Even with a busy job, I try to balance running, work and family life.

To train in the morning, I developed the habit of going to bed early and waking early.

My life has become simpler.

I do not need much nightlife, and I do not feel that I am missing out.

Instead, this way of living keeps me energetic and gives me a calmer state of mind.

Of course, running is not enjoyable and easy every day.

Sometimes training is tiring, sometimes I am not in good shape, and sometimes results stall.

But these experiences have gradually taught me to accept the ups and downs of the process and to value every hard-won improvement.

If I did not run, I do not know what I would be like today.

Perhaps I would still be the same person who sat in an office all day, weighed 200 jin and had little desire to exercise.

Today, I can use my own feet to reach many places that I once could only imagine.

What a fortunate thing that is.

Looking back over the past ten years, the people I most want to thank are those who accompanied and helped me along the way.

Thank you, Liu Ying.

A friend of 20 years, from first encouraging me to run to later helping me train systematically, she has always been an important guide on my running path.

Thank you to the coaches and runners of Yuandadu Champions Club.

Becoming the first member of Yuandadu Shenzhen Club and being called "First Brother" is a connection I have always treasured.

Thank you to my wife, Bingbing.

Whether it was the Chinese flag handed to me before the Berlin finish, or her presence beside the courses in London and Boston, these are among the warmest parts of my marathon memories.

Thank you also to my parents, family and friends who supported me throughout.

Without everyone's encouragement and companionship, my marathon journey would not have been so wonderful.

Finally, I want to thank the version of myself ten years ago who was willing to take the first step.

In 2016, I completed 50 runs in 20 weeks.

At the time, every number was a reminder not to give up.

Ten years later, I am still numbering my runs.

It is just that every number now carries a city, an experience, a group of friends, and a version of myself that keeps growing.

The eight stars are a precious gift from these ten years.

But they are not the finish line of my running.

I will keep running.

I will keep racing, exploring new places, learning about scientific training and trying to break through my own limits.

As for how fast or how far I can run in the future, I do not know.

But one thing I am increasingly certain of:

Change in life does not need to begin with some grand dream.

It may be a single morning run, a small check-in, or the willingness to take another step when the finish is out of sight.

The version of me ten years ago could not imagine today.

Perhaps the version of me ten years from now will also surprise the person I am today.

42.195 kilometres has a finish line, but personal growth does not.

Written in November 2026 to mark my ten years of running.
